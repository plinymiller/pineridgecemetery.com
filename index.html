Pine Ridge Cemetery

The old Moody burying ground · in use since the 1840s

A community project by Saranac Lake families and volunteers. Not the official site of the Pine Ridge Cemetery Association.

Twelve terraced acres above River Street hold the village's whole story: the first settlers, the guides and hotel keepers, the doctors who fought tuberculosis, and the patients from every corner of the world who came here hoping to get well.

History

A farm knoll that became the village's memory

Jacob Smith Moody brought his family to this valley in 1819, the first settlers of what became Saranac Lake. When the family needed a burial place they chose a pine-covered knoll on their own farm, close to the house on River Street, and they let any neighbor who needed a grave use it without charge. For generations it was simply the Moody Cemetery.

Then the village changed. Dr. Edward Livingston Trudeau opened his sanatorium in 1884, and people with tuberculosis arrived from across the country and overseas. Many recovered. Many did not, and those who could not be sent home on the night train were buried here, some in graves that never received a stone. A patient curing in a cottage on Clinton Avenue, the poet Adelaide Crapsey, looked down on these graves from her window and wrote about them.

The hillside holds a Catholic ground (old St. Bernard's), a Jewish ground (the Hebrew Memorial Cemetery) and the old Protestant lots side by side. Historic Saranac Lake calls that unusual for a remote mountain village of the time. Stone terrace walls, laid in several styles, hold the slope together.

  1. 1819

    The Moody family settles the farm that includes today's cemetery. Pine Street follows the old Northwest Bay Road.

  2. 1843

    Mary Ann Reynolds, age seven, is buried. Hers is the oldest marked grave.

  3. 1852

    Sally Moody, Jacob's wife, becomes the first of the family laid on the knoll.

  4. 1884

    Trudeau's sanatorium opens. In 1892 it buys lots here for eight of its patients.

  5. 1894

    St. Bernard's parish acquires an acre and a half north of the Moody plots. Benjamin Moody keeps the right to cut hay there for two more years.

  6. 1916

    Pine Ridge Cemetery incorporates and buys the old Moody ground. The directors first meet in the Adirondack National Bank building.

  7. 1919

    The Hebrew Memorial Cemetery is dedicated with sixteen plots and a bronze plaque.

  8. 1925

    Architects Scopes and Feustmann design a winter vault for thirty, where the dead waited for the ground to thaw in May. A stone and iron gateway goes up on River Street.

  9. 1940

    The National Vaudeville Artists Lodge buys eight plots for its patients.

  10. 1942–45

    Norwegian merchant sailors, stranded when Germany invaded their country and sent here to cure, are buried together near the vault.

  11. Today

    Still an active cemetery, owned by an association of lot holders and families and kept up largely by volunteers.
